# Terpenes in Medical Cannabis

Terpenes are aromatic compounds found in cannabis and many other plants. They contribute to the flavour and scent of medical cannabis flowers and are being researched for their possible biological effects.

This page provides an overview of common terpenes, their flavour and scent profiles, approximate boiling points, potential health benefits being investigated, and subjective effects you can record after vaping.

The information is intended to support personal research into medical cannabis for bone pain, nerve pain, anxiety, depression and seasonal affective disorder (SAD). The effects of an individual terpene are not reliably predictable, and the evidence for many proposed health benefits remains limited.

## Understanding Terpenes

Terpenes are naturally occurring compounds responsible for many of the distinctive aromas found in plants. In cannabis, they contribute to the differences in aroma and flavour between flowers.

Terpenes are sometimes suggested as a way to predict whether a cannabis flower will be relaxing, uplifting, energising or sedating. However, there is not enough reliable clinical evidence to make these predictions confidently.

The effects of medical cannabis may depend on several interacting factors, including:

- THC and CBD concentrations.
- The amounts and combinations of terpenes and other compounds.
- The dose inhaled.
- The temperature and method of administration.
- Individual sensitivity and tolerance.
- Time of day, sleep quality, mood and pain levels.
- Other medication and health conditions.

For this reason, the purpose of this page is to identify patterns in personal experience rather than assume that a particular terpene will produce a specific effect.

### Potential areas of health research

Terpenes are being investigated for several possible biological effects, including:

- Pain modulation.
- Inflammatory processes.
- Anxiety-related effects.
- Effects on mood and stress responses.
- Antioxidant activity.
- Effects on the nervous system.

Much of this research comes from laboratory experiments and animal studies. Results from these studies do not establish that inhaling a cannabis flower containing a particular terpene will provide the same effect in humans.

## Vaping Temperatures

Terpenes have different physical properties, including different boiling points. However, **a terpene's boiling point is not the same as the temperature at which it begins to evaporate from cannabis flower**.

A dry-herb vaporiser heats a mixture of plant material and compounds. It does not selectively release one terpene at a time. Actual release depends on the flower, the device, airflow, heating method and other conditions.

The temperatures below are approximate reference values for pure compounds, not recommended vaporiser settings.

|---|---:|
| Alpha-pinene | 155°C |
| Sabinene | 163°C |
| Beta-pinene | 166°C |
| Myrcene | 168°C |
| Limonene | 176°C |
| Terpinolene | 185°C |
| Linalool | 198°C |
| Fenchol | Around 200°C |
| Alpha-terpineol | Around 217°C |
| Beta-caryophyllene | Around 260°C |
| Nerolidol | Around 260°C |

| Phytol | Above 300°C |
| Ocimene | Varies by isomer |

*Values are approximate and may vary between references and chemical isomers.*

### Practical considerations

- A terpene may evaporate below its boiling point.
- A vaporiser's displayed temperature may not precisely match the temperature of the flower.
- Higher settings may change the flavour and harshness of the vapour.
- The temperature alone cannot reliably predict which terpenes you inhale or the effects you experience.
- Avoid increasing the temperature simply to target a terpene with a higher boiling point.
- Follow the advice provided by your prescribing clinician or dispensing pharmacy and the instructions for your vaporiser.

When comparing products, it is more useful to record the device temperature and your actual response than to assume that a particular temperature produces a particular therapeutic effect.

## Individual Terpene Profiles

The following profiles summarise common aroma descriptions and areas of scientific interest. The subjective effects are possibilities to observe and record, not established effects of the individual terpene.

### 1. Myrcene

**Typical aroma:** Earthy, musky, herbal and sometimes clove-like.

**Flavour profile:** Earthy, herbal and slightly peppery.

**Approximate boiling point:** 168°C.

**Potential health benefits under investigation:**

- Possible effects on inflammatory processes.
- Possible interactions with pain-related pathways.
- Possible effects on the nervous system.

Myrcene is often associated with relaxing or sedating cannabis products. However, there is insufficient clinical evidence to conclude that myrcene itself reliably causes sedation or improves sleep when inhaled in cannabis flower.

**Subjective effects to observe:**

- Feeling relaxed or physically at ease.
- Sleepiness or reduced alertness.
- Reduced or unchanged bone pain.
- Reduced or unchanged nerve pain.
- Changes in motivation or concentration.
- Any unwanted mental fog or fatigue.

**Relevance to my research:** Record whether products containing myrcene appear to be associated with relaxation, pain relief or sleepiness, particularly when comparing daytime and evening use.

### 2. Limonene

**Typical aroma:** Lemon, orange and other citrus fruits.

**Flavour profile:** Citrus, fresh, sweet and sometimes slightly sharp.

**Approximate boiling point:** 176°C.

**Potential health benefits under investigation:**

- Possible effects on anxiety-related processes.
- Possible effects on mood and stress responses.
- Antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity in experimental research.

Limonene is often described as uplifting in cannabis discussions. This should not be treated as a reliable prediction of improved mood or reduced depression in a patient.

**Subjective effects to observe:**

- Improved or unchanged mood.
- Reduced or increased anxiety.
- Greater motivation or enjoyment.
- Improved or reduced concentration.
- Increased alertness or unwanted restlessness.
- Any change in sleepiness.

**Relevance to my research:** Observe whether products with a citrus aroma are associated with changes in depression, anxiety, motivation or daytime functioning.

### 3. Beta-caryophyllene

**Typical aroma:** Black pepper, spice, wood and sometimes cloves.

**Flavour profile:** Peppery, woody, spicy and warm.

**Approximate boiling point:** Around 260°C.

**Potential health benefits under investigation:**

- Interaction with the CB2 cannabinoid receptor.
- Possible effects on inflammatory processes.
- Possible relevance to pain-related pathways.

Beta-caryophyllene is unusual among common terpenes because it can interact with CB2 receptors. This is scientifically interesting, but it does not establish that a flower containing beta-caryophyllene will provide clinically meaningful pain relief.

**Subjective effects to observe:**

- Changes in bone pain.
- Changes in nerve pain.
- Feeling physically comfortable or relaxed.
- Changes in anxiety or mood.
- Sleepiness or alertness.
- Any unwanted effects.

**Relevance to my research:** Compare pain scores before and after use and record any next-day changes. Do not assume that a product's beta-caryophyllene content explains a change in pain.

### 4. Linalool

**Typical aroma:** Lavender, flowers and sweet herbs.

**Flavour profile:** Floral, sweet and sometimes slightly spicy.

**Approximate boiling point:** 198°C.

**Potential health benefits under investigation:**

- Possible anxiety-related effects.
- Possible effects on inflammatory processes.
- Possible effects on pain-related pathways.
- Possible effects on the nervous system.

Linalool is frequently associated with lavender and relaxation. Evidence from studies of isolated linalool or lavender preparations does not necessarily apply to inhaled medical cannabis.

**Subjective effects to observe:**

- Feeling calm or relaxed.
- Reduced or increased anxiety.
- Changes in mood or depressive symptoms.
- Sleepiness or sedation.
- Improved or impaired concentration.
- Changes in pain or muscle tension.

**Relevance to my research:** Record whether products with a floral or lavender-like aroma appear to be associated with relaxation, anxiety changes or unwanted daytime sleepiness.

### 5. Alpha-pinene

**Typical aroma:** Pine needles, resin and woodland.

**Flavour profile:** Fresh, pine-like, resinous and slightly sharp.

**Approximate boiling point:** 155°C.

**Potential health benefits under investigation:**

- Possible anti-inflammatory effects.
- Possible effects on the nervous system.
- Possible effects on cognitive processes in experimental research.

Alpha-pinene is sometimes claimed to improve alertness or counteract THC-related memory impairment. These claims should not be relied upon as established clinical effects.

**Subjective effects to observe:**

- Mental clarity or mental fog.
- Concentration and attention.
- Alertness or sleepiness.
- Changes in mood and motivation.
- Changes in anxiety.
- Any unwanted racing thoughts or restlessness.

**Relevance to my research:** This terpene may be worth tracking when assessing daytime products, particularly if the goal is to improve mood and functioning without excessive sedation.

### 6. Beta-pinene

**Typical aroma:** Pine, wood and fresh herbs.

**Flavour profile:** Woody, herbal, pine-like and sometimes earthy.

**Approximate boiling point:** 166°C.

**Potential health benefits under investigation:**

- Possible anti-inflammatory effects.
- Possible antioxidant activity.
- Other experimental biological effects.

Beta-pinene is related to alpha-pinene, but the two compounds are chemically distinct. Their effects should not automatically be assumed to be identical.

**Subjective effects to observe:**

- Alertness.
- Concentration.
- Mental clarity.
- Relaxation.
- Changes in anxiety or mood.
- Any unwanted stimulation or sedation.

**Relevance to my research:** Record the actual effects rather than assuming that a pine-like aroma indicates an energising product.

### 7. Terpinolene

**Typical aroma:** Fresh herbs, pine, citrus and sometimes flowers.

**Flavour profile:** Herbal, citrus-like, slightly sweet and pine-like.

**Approximate boiling point:** 185°C.

**Potential health benefits under investigation:**

- Antioxidant activity in experimental studies.
- Possible effects on inflammatory processes.
- Other experimental biological effects.

Terpinolene is sometimes associated with stimulating or uplifting cannabis products. There is insufficient evidence to use its presence as a reliable predictor of energy, mood or concentration.

**Subjective effects to observe:**

- Alertness or sleepiness.
- Changes in anxiety.
- Any unwanted restlessness.

**Relevance to my research:** Record whether products containing terpinolene appear to support daytime functioning or instead cause anxiety, fatigue or other unwanted effects.

### 8. Humulene

**Typical aroma:** Hops, wood, earth and herbs.

**Approximate boiling point:** Around 275°C.

**Potential health benefits under investigation:**

- Possible anti-inflammatory effects.

Humulene is also found in hops and other plants. Its presence in cannabis does not establish that the flower will reduce pain or inflammation.

**Subjective effects to observe:**

- Sleepiness or fatigue.
- Any unwanted effects.

**Relevance to my research:** Record pain and movement-related changes alongside mood and daily functioning.

Ocimene exists in several chemical forms, so a single boiling-point figure is not appropriate for every form.

### 10. Bisabolol

**Typical aroma:** Chamomile, flowers and soft sweetness.

**Flavour profile:** Mild, floral, sweet and sometimes slightly woody.

**Approximate boiling point:** Around 310°C.

**Potential health benefits under investigation:**

- Possible anti-inflammatory effects.
- Possible effects on irritation and inflammatory pathways.

Bisabolol is found in plants including chamomile. Research into isolated bisabolol does not establish the effects of cannabis flowers that contain it.

- Any unwanted effects.

### 11. Nerolidol

**Typical aroma:** Woody, floral and fresh.

**Flavour profile:** Woody, floral, slightly citrus-like and sometimes reminiscent of fresh bark.

**Approximate boiling point:** Around 260°C, depending on the form and reference.

**Potential health benefits under investigation:**

- Possible anti-inflammatory effects.
- Possible effects on the nervous system.
- Other experimental biological effects.

Nerolidol is a sesquiterpene found in several plants. Some research has examined its biological activity, but clinical evidence for benefits from inhaled cannabis containing nerolidol is limited.

- Relaxation.

## How to Interpret My Findings

Personal observations can help identify which products seem to suit your needs. However, they cannot establish that a particular terpene caused a benefit or unwanted effect.

### Look for repeated patterns

A single experience may be affected by sleep, stress, pain severity, expectations, dose or other factors. Look for patterns across several uses and, where possible, compare similar circumstances.

### Separate aroma from effect

A product's smell and taste may help you recognise it, but aroma alone does not establish its chemical composition or likely medical effects. Laboratory terpene results, when available, provide more reliable information about composition.

### Consider the whole product

Two flowers with similar terpene profiles may produce different experiences because they differ in THC, CBD, other compounds, dose or individual response.

The proposed **entourage effect** suggests that cannabis compounds may influence one another's effects. Although this is an area of research, the extent and clinical significance of these interactions remain uncertain. A terpene profile should not be treated as a proven formula for a particular medical outcome.

### Track mental and physical wellbeing separately

For this research, record mental and physical outcomes independently. A product might appear helpful for pain but worsen concentration or anxiety. Another might improve relaxation but cause too much daytime sleepiness.

For depression and SAD, consider whether any perceived improvement lasts beyond the immediate effects of vaping and whether it translates into better daily functioning. Cannabis should not replace established mental health treatment without discussion with your clinician.

### Consider the timing of effects

Record immediate effects separately from later and next-day observations. If evening use appears to coincide with lower pain the following day, document the pattern, but remember that personal observations alone cannot establish causation.

### Safety

THC-containing cannabis can affect attention, memory, judgement, coordination and reaction time. It can also cause anxiety, paranoia, dizziness, confusion or sleepiness. Do not drive or operate machinery while impaired, and follow current UK legal requirements and your prescribing clinician's advice.

If a product consistently worsens mood, anxiety, concentration or other symptoms, discuss the pattern with your prescribing clinician.
